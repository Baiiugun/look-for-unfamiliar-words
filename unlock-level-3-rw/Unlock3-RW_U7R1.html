THRIFTING GOES GLOBAL

1 In January 2023, 19-year-old Jacklyn Wells posted the results of her latest shopping trip on TikTok, where she has more than 400,000 followers. Two months later, more than six million people had viewed her post, and quite a few of them were very angry. Why were so many people interested in her shopping trip? And why were some of them upset about it? Wells is a thrifter and a reseller, and both have become controversial. Thrifting means shopping in stores that sell second-hand (already used) clothing and other items. In general, this clothing is inexpensive. Resellers buy second-hand clothes and then sell them online on sites such as Depop and Poshmark, usually at a much higher price.

2 Buying second-hand clothing has not always been in fashion. Used-clothing stores first appeared in the 19th century, and they were mostly for poor people. People who had to shop in these stores often felt ashamed. Recently however, internet influencers like Wells have made thrifting very popular, and this trend is expected to continue. The global second-hand clothing market is growing three times faster than the general clothing market. It is expected to reach $300 billion per year by 2026, and about half of those sales will be online (See Figure 1). Today, many thrifters are not poor, and they don’t need to buy second-hand clothing. For them, thrifting is a hobby, and sometimes, a business. They enjoy the challenge of finding unusual and attractive items at low prices. Then they share the results online with their followers, and, like Wells, they often resell.

3 Some people are upset about this trend. They say that new thrifters are taking items away from people who don’t have enough money to buy new clothes. In addition, the popularity of thrifting has led to higher prices for everyone. Although it is true that new thrifters often buy the most expensive brands of clothing, this probably makes very little difference in the market. The volume of second-hand clothing is so high that only about 20% is sold online or in shops. The rest is shipped to developing countries or thrown away.

4 Many consumers also choose second-hand clothes instead of new because they believe it is a more sustainable option. Giving old clothes a second life saves resources compared to manufacturing new clothes. Thrifting can be a good sustainability strategy. However, it is no longer a good strategy when it becomes a reason to waste. A shopper may hesitate before spending $200 on a new jacket, but it’s much easier to buy five second-hand jackets at the same price. As a result, thrifting may encourage people to buy much more than they need, especially if they think they are helping the planet!

5 Both economic and sustainability experts say the best strategy is to buy only what you need, new or second-hand. Before you go shopping, first look in your wardrobe. The best option is to wear what you already own.

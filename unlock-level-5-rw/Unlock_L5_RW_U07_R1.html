Unit 07 · Health Sciences

Reading 1

AIR POLLUTION AND PUBLIC HEALTH

1 It may be a familiar sight: a dull haze lingers over a city. The haze has a brownish quality and limits visibility, even as people go about their daily lives. While this may be a common scenario in some cities, it is important to know that air pollution in every form, whether visible as smog (a combination of the words smoke and fog) or invisible, poses a global threat to human health. Smog may not be present in every city, but data from the World Health Organization indicates that 99% of the world’s population breathes polluted air. Globally, air pollution is one of the leading causes of chronic disease and premature death; in some countries, it contributes to up to 10% of all deaths. There are several causes of air pollution, though they all stem from human activity.

2 Fossil fuels are energy sources that are extracted from plants and animals that have been fossilized – or preserved in rock – for hundreds of thousands of years or more. These extracts are converted into such energy sources as electricity, heat and gasoline. Everything from cars to homes to small businesses to gigantic industries relies on fossil fuels to generate energy. However, fossil fuels contain high levels of carbon, allowing carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases to be emitted into the atmosphere. This creates air pollution.

3 How, then, does air pollution impact health? Take one example: fossil fuel powered vehicles. Cars, trucks and buses release poisonous chemicals into the air in the form of particulate matter, which is inhaled into the body by the simple act of breathing. And because the matter is smaller than a grain of sand, it can easily make its way into the bloodstream or lungs. This triggers adverse effects, including breathing difficulties and lung infections. People with existing health conditions are especially vulnerable to complications that arise from air pollution. Prolonged exposure can lead to lung cancer, heart disease and serious respiratory illnesses. In short, air pollution impacts the global mortality rate, a fact that should raise the alarm for immediate action to be taken.

4 Another cause of air pollution is wildfires. Across the globe, climate change is evident. Temperatures are rising, resulting in warmer seasons that last longer and a ground that is drier than ever. One spark, whether from a carelessly thrown cigarette, a smouldering¹ campfire or a strike of lightning, can ignite a wildfire. The resulting smoke carries toxic particulate matter for potentially thousands of kilometres, exposing countless people to air pollution. Wildfire smoke also releases carbon monoxide. Breathing in this poisonous gas prevents sufficient oxygen from reaching the body’s organs and tissues. Consequences include dizziness and nausea. People also report feeling fatigue or ‘brain fog’ during wildfire season. One study has found that particulate matter can impact the attention span after only a few hours of exposure.

5 It is imperative that action be taken to counter the effects of air pollution, it is a health hazard for all humankind, but especially problematic for vulnerable populations. A California study found that children who were exposed to the smoky air of wildfires suffered from coughing, bronchitis, colds and other respiratory ailments. A 2020 report from the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air concluded that, as a result of air pollution, 16 million children live with asthma.

6 Reducing air pollution is key to improving public health. Countries like the United States, where 81% of the total energy is derived from fossil fuels, must phase out non-renewable energy sources and instead utilize sustainable sources such as solar and wind power. In the face of recent wildfires, reducing wildfires is urgent. In 2023 alone, over 800,000 acres of land burned in Chile, Canada experienced its most devastating fires in recorded history, and Spain reported that their wildfire season now lasts longer than ever. The United Nations Environment Programme provides a grim warning, predicting that extreme wildfires will have increased by up to 50% by the end of the 21st century.

7 Air pollution is closely intertwined with public health; therefore, reducing the former will improve the latter. For this reason, it is essential to take steps now, especially if we want future generations to not only survive but thrive. Children should be able to grow up breathing clean air rather than being at the mercy of a dying planet.

¹smouldering (v) burning slowly; producing smoke but no flames

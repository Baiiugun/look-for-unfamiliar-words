Reading 2

HOW OCEAN POLLUTION AFFECTS HEALTH

1 In many ways, the world's oceans pose a great mystery. Covering more than 70% of the earth, they remain largely unexplored and unseen by human eyes. Nevertheless, oceans around the globe are polluted by human activity. And while it may be easy to blame it on the fishing or shipping industries or on beaches piled with rubbish by too many tourists, the truth is that 80% of marine pollution originates on land. It is brought about by air pollution from urban factories, disease-carrying bacteria from agricultural regions and the plastic we use on a day-to-day basis. These pollutants not only harm marine life, but also promote the transmission of disease to humans. Ocean pollution is facilitating a public health crisis.

2 Plastic waste is the leading cause of sea pollution. Because it is lightweight and durable, plastic is used in countless ways in almost every aspect of our lives. To illustrate its pervasiveness, the non-profit Plastic Oceans International estimates that, around the world, one million plastic bags are used every sixty seconds. Many are then thrown away. Research shows that, as of 2019, 460 million tonnes of plastic are produced per year and an astounding 0.5% of that finds its way into the ocean. The problem is so severe that Nikola Simpson of the United Nation's Development Programme warns that, if we don't reduce our reliance on plastic, plastic fragments may outnumber the fish in the sea by the middle of the twenty-first century.

3 How does plastic find its way to the ocean? Research suggests it is primarily carried by rivers. Imagine, for example, a plastic water bottle is inadequately disposed of in Budapest. That the landlocked city is 500 kilometres away from the coast makes no difference; its proximity to a river does. The river carries it out to sea. Along the way, the plastic decomposes, absorbing pesticides and other pollutants that were already present in the water. The plastic eventually breaks down into microplastics, categorized as small that are less than 5 millimetres in length, fragments enough for fish and shellfish to consume. Humans then eat the contaminated fish, allowing our bodies to also take in those tiny toxic fragments. Research on the consequences of ingesting microplastics, which has been called 'the silent menace', points to cause for concern. A 2023 study in Environment & Health found evidence that microplastics can damage the liver, intestines and brain; they may also damage DNA. Additional research published in 2023 for the California State Legislature listed endocrine, cognitive and reproductive problems, as well as respiratory illnesses and cancer, as some of the health hazards of microplastics.

4 Another dangerous ocean pollutant is mercury, a naturally occurring element that comes from coal. When coal is burned as an energy source, mercury settles into the atmosphere. With the help of rain or snow, it is washed into rivers and carried to the ocean. Over time, fish may accumulate large amounts of mercury in their systems, which is then delivered to humans via the contaminated fish. As a neurotoxin, mercury can damage the nervous system. It may also harm the digestive and immune systems, as well as the lungs, kidneys, skin and eyes. Mercury poisoning puts adults at risk of incurable diseases such as heart disease and dementia. Tragically, the danger is not confined to adults. If an expectant mother consumes mercury, it can harm the developing brain of her unborn baby. The World Health Organization includes mercury in their top ten chemicals of public health concern.

5 Work must be done to reduce ocean pollution; however, the trend is heading in the wrong direction. Based on a 2023 study in the journal PLOS One, there has been a surge in ocean plastic pollution since 2005; that research estimated that approximately 171 trillion plastic particles are now present in the world's oceans. The United Nations Environment Programme is pushing for an 80% reduction in plastic use over the next 20 years. On the part of consumers, reusing and recycling are major steps toward reaching that goal. However, real change must occur at the root. Our global reliance on plastic must be minimized, forcing a decrease in production. Once that happens, we can begin the work of eradicating the illnesses that arise as a result.
